What can a fitness test tell you that your smartwatch can't?

A VO2 max test and a smartwatch estimate answer different questions. Here's what direct gas-exchange testing reveals that a wearable structurally can't, and when it's worth the cost.

A VO2 max test vs. a smartwatch estimate comes down to one basic difference: direct measurement versus inference. VO2 is the volume of oxygen your body takes up and uses; VO2max is the highest rate it can reach during hard exercise. Your watch infers that number from heart rate and pace. A lab test measures it directly, and along the way it produces several numbers your watch never sees at all.

We covered how watches estimate VO2max, and how far off that estimate can run, in our last post. This one is about the other side: what a lab test adds that a wearable structurally can't provide, and when that gap is worth paying for.

Key takeaways
  • A watch gives you one estimated number built from an algorithm.
  • A lab test gives you a measured number plus your ventilatory thresholds, a check on whether you actually hit your max, and your own heart-rate-to-effort relationship instead of a generic formula.
  • Whether that's worth paying for depends on what you're using the number for, not on one tool being universally better than the other.

What your watch gives you: one estimated number

A consumer wearable estimates VO2max from your heart rate at a given pace, or from heart rate at rest on some devices, using an algorithm built on relationships that hold on average across groups of people. Workout-based estimates are accurate on average, but they can be off by around 10 mL/kg/min for any one person. That's the ceiling of what the number can tell you: one estimate, with no breakdown of how your body got there, and no way to tell you whether today's reading reflects a real change in fitness or just a hot, hilly week.

What a lab test actually measures

A lab test puts you on a treadmill or bike and steps up the effort in stages, while a gas analyzer measures the oxygen you breathe in and the carbon dioxide you breathe out, breath by breath, until you reach your limit. This kind of staged, rising-effort protocol is usually called a graded exercise test, and the point of grading it rather than just asking you to run as hard as possible is that it maps your whole effort range, not just the top of it. That continuous stream of gas-exchange data is the real difference. It isn't one number at the end, it's a curve, and that curve is where everything else in this post comes from.

Three things a lab test can show you that a watch can't

Your own ventilatory thresholds, not just a peak number

As effort climbs, your breathing shifts through recognizable stages: a first point where it starts to feel noticeably harder to hold a conversation, and a second where you can no longer sustain the pace for long. Exercise physiologists call these the first and second ventilatory thresholds, and they mark the boundaries between easy, moderately hard, and very hard exercise. A 1986 paper by Beaver, Wasserman and Whipp set out a widely used way to spot these shifts in gas-exchange data during a graded test, exactly the kind of data only a lab test collects. A smartwatch, which sees only heart rate and pace, has no way to detect this directly.

A 2024 systematic review notes that ventilatory and lactate thresholds correlate closely but aren't always considered identical, so it's fairer to treat VT1 and VT2 as well-supported estimates of where those transitions happen, not an exact line painted on your body.

Confirmation that you reached a true maximal effort

In our view, this is the most underrated of the three, because it answers a basic question a watch can't: did you actually give a maximal effort, or just a hard one? Labs commonly use a secondary marker, the respiratory exchange ratio (the ratio of carbon dioxide produced to oxygen consumed), alongside the VO2 curve itself to check this. But even that check isn't one fixed number: a 2020 study proposed a cutoff of 1.13 for adults aged 20 to 39 and 1.10 for ages 40 to 59, derived from data across the adult lifespan. We'd call that a reminder that "maximal" is itself a judgment call researchers are still refining, not a settled line. A watch has no way to check whether you reached your ceiling at all, let alone confirm it with a secondary marker.

Your actual heart-rate-to-effort relationship, not a generic formula

Most watches set training zones off a formula like 220 minus your age, or a step up from it. Research on these formulas doesn't support them as precise: a widely cited 2001 study pooling data from over 18,000 people found a better-fitting formula, 208 minus 0.7 times age, and that the commonly used 220-minus-age formula carries real error, particularly in older adults.

Even a better formula is still a formula: it predicts what's typical for your age, not what's true for you. A lab test measures your own heart rate at your own thresholds directly, rather than assuming it from a population curve. In a study of 26 elite cyclists, researchers found that a given percentage of max heart rate consistently corresponded to a higher percentage of VO2max than the standard population-based scale predicted. It's a small, elite-only sample, so we wouldn't generalize it to every reader, but we'd read it as a caution against trusting a generic zone chart for anyone training seriously, and less of a concern if you're just exercising for general fitness.

What your watch is still good for

None of this makes your watch useless. We covered why in our last post: it's a reasonable tool for noticing week-to-week trends, cheap to use every single day, and it doesn't require booking an appointment. That post also laid out a simple check for whether a reading is a real change or just noise: look at the direction over several weeks, compare similar conditions, and be skeptical of a one- or two-point move either way. None of that requires a lab test. A lab test can't give you daily data at all, and for someone who mostly wants to know whether this month looks better than last month, a watch read for trend rather than precision does the job.

Is a VO2 max test worth it?

Our take: it depends on what you're using the number for, not on one tool being better across the board. Someone setting training zones for a structured block, or preparing for a specific race, may benefit from a direct measurement, since generic zone formulas and estimate-based numbers leave real room for error, as the sections above show. Someone who just wants to see whether they're trending up or down over a season probably doesn't need one: a watch, read for trend rather than precision, does that job reasonably well.

If you already track a lot of data and still feel unsure what to act on, more numbers usually aren't the fix. We opened this series by asking whether collecting fitness data and understanding it are the same thing, and they aren't: a lab test adds real detail a watch structurally can't produce, but that detail only helps once you know which question you're using it to answer. "What are my zones for this training block" is a question a lab test answers well. "Am I getting fitter this month" usually isn't, and a watch trend already covers it.

Cost and access are real parts of this decision too; a lab test is a paid appointment at a facility that has the equipment, not something you can do on a whim. A later post in this series covers why more people don't get one despite its value, so we won't relitigate that here.

The bottom line

A smartwatch and a lab test are different tools built for different questions. The watch is built to notice change over time, cheaply and often. The lab test is built to measure where your body actually makes its biggest transitions, and to tell you, with more certainty than an estimate can offer, whether you truly hit your limit. Use the watch to ask whether you're trending the right way. Use a lab test when a more specific question, like what your actual zones are or whether you really maxed out, matters enough to you to pay for it.

FAQ

What's the difference between a VO2 max test and a smartwatch estimate?

A lab test measures your oxygen uptake directly through gas exchange and gives you ventilatory thresholds and a maximal-effort check along the way. A smartwatch estimates VO2max from heart rate and pace with a population-based algorithm and gives you one number, with no threshold or effort-confirmation data behind it.

What does a VO2 max test actually involve?

You exercise on a treadmill or bike while wearing a mask connected to a gas analyzer, with the effort stepped up in stages until you reach your limit. The device measures the oxygen and carbon dioxide in every breath along the way, which is how it can produce a full curve rather than a single number.

Do I need a lab VO2 max test to train effectively?

No. General training can be guided reasonably well by a watch read for trends rather than for precise accuracy. A lab test may benefit people setting zones for a structured training block, or who want their own measured thresholds instead of a formula-based estimate.

How often should you get a VO2 max test?

There's no single answer; it depends on how your training changes. Testing before and after a focused training block is a common approach among athletes who use lab testing at all.

Can a smartwatch replace a lab test?

Not for what a lab test does specifically: confirming maximal effort and finding your own thresholds. For tracking whether your fitness is trending up or down over weeks, a watch is a reasonable, far cheaper substitute.
