Peak Performance: The Tour de France Guide to VO2 Max

The Ultimate Test of Human Endurance

The Tour de France stands as the pinnacle of professional cycling, an annual odyssey that has defined the limits of human capability since its inception in 1903. What began as a six-stage venture covering 2,428 kilometres has evolved into a gruelling 21-stage masterpiece of suffering, spanning roughly 3,300–3,500 kilometres across the most unforgiving mountain passes in Europe [1]. It remains the world's most coveted race and the definitive test of endurance.

Strategy, team tactics, and psychological resilience all matter, and so does physiology. Reviews of endurance performance point to three main factors: VO2 max, lactate threshold, and efficiency [5]. VO2 max, often described as the "engine" of cycling performance, sets an upper limit on endurance performance [6].

The Science of VO2 Max

In exercise physiology, VO2 max is defined as the maximal oxygen uptake, the highest rate at which an individual can transport and utilize oxygen during incremental exercise. It is a standard indicator of aerobic fitness.

The heart pumps oxygenated blood to the working muscles, and Bassett and Howley view that oxygen delivery as the primary limit on VO2 max [6]. A high VO2 max doesn't settle performance alone: how efficiently a rider uses oxygen, and how much of their VO2 max they can sustain, also matter [5, 6].

Key Metric: VO2 Max. VO2 max is measured in ml/kg/min (millilitres of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute). Expressing it relative to body mass makes it easier to compare riders of different sizes.

Approximate ranges for general orientation, not formal standards.

Published figures offer a window into elite physiology. A published lab case study of a multiple Tour winner, identified in press coverage as Chris Froome, reported about 84 ml/kg/min [7, 12]. More recently, a researcher used publicly available climbing data to estimate that Tadej Pogačar's figure could be as high as 96 ml/kg/min. That is a modelled estimate, not a lab test [2]. The highest score on Top End Sports' list of world-best values is 97.5 ml/kg/min, recorded by Norwegian cyclist Oskar Svendsen at age 18, and the site notes that not all reported scores have been independently confirmed [3].

Crucially, VO2 max is a ceiling that athletes can't hold for extended periods [6]. Professional road cyclists spend prolonged periods, such as time trials and high mountain ascents, at about 90% of it [8].

How to Measure Your Aerobic Ceiling

A lab test measures VO2 max directly. A field formula can only estimate it.

Field Estimation (The Uth Formula). For a rough estimate at home, Danish researchers Uth, Sørensen, Overgaard, and Pedersen proposed the Heart Rate Ratio Method, which they tested in 46 well-trained men aged 21 to 51 [4]:

VO2 max ≈ 15.3 × (HRmax ÷ HRrest)
  • Measured maximum heart rate: The standard error of the estimate was about 4.5% [4].
  • Age-predicted maximum heart rate: The standard error rose to about 7.8% [4].
  • Limit: The authors say its use in other groups awaits direct validation [4].

What Elite Training Can and Can't Tell the Rest of Us

Descriptive studies of competitive endurance athletes show about 80% of sessions at low intensity and about 20% dominated by high-intensity work [10]. That describes what those athletes do. It is not a plan for anyone else, so take training decisions to a qualified coach.

  • Low-Intensity Training (Zone 2): Its role in directly driving mitochondrial biogenesis is debated. A 2025 narrative review found the evidence does not support Zone 2 as the optimal intensity for improving mitochondrial or fat-oxidative capacity, and that higher intensities appear more effective for those adaptations [9].
  • Interval Training (HIIT): In a trial of 40 moderately trained men, 4x4 intervals improved VO2 max more than the same total work at lower intensities, and the changes corresponded with changes in stroke volume [11].
  • Body Mass and the Number: Because ml/kg/min is expressed per kilogram, the figure changes with body mass as well as with oxygen uptake. That is arithmetic, not a reason to change your weight.

Beyond Race Day

You don't need to race the Tour to find your numbers useful. A lab test or estimate can give you a reference point for your aerobic fitness, as long as you allow for the error in any estimate.

Tracking Your Progress

Few riders will approach the figures above. Consistent measurement can still help you compare your own numbers over time.
